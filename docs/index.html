Teaching Demo: The Business of Show Business

Introduction

In this project, you will play the role of a Hollywood development executive; that is, you are the executive in charge of coming up with new movie ideas. Historically, development executives would source the ‘life rights’ necessary to make “based on a true story” movies, would secure production options on promising new novels, and would partner with owners of established intellectual property (IP) to develop movie adaptations. Recently, however, the development process has been criticized by Hollywood insiders and audiences alike for over-reliance on rote sequels. Our goal is to develop a set of data-driven ideas for new movies. Before doing so, however, we will dive into Hollywood history to identify key characteristics of successful movies, to identify successful filmmakers and actors, and to examine some of Hollywood’s most famous flops.

Course Context

STA9750

STA9750, the course from which this lesson was adapted, is a core requirement of the MS in Statistics and MS in Quantitative Methods & Modeling (QMM) programs offered by the Department of Information Systems and Statistics (ISS) within the Zicklin School. STA9750 is also an elective commonly taken by students in our MS in Business Analytics and AI program and ‘analytics-flavored’ tracks in other Zicklin programs. This is an intense one-semester course that teaches students the most important computational tools for working data scientists and business analysts.

In our LLM moment, STA9750 has been restructured as an ‘applied data analysis’ course, focusing on the habits and practices of effective data analysis and de-emphasizing traditional ‘programming’ fundamentals. As the marginal cost of writing code decreases, the relative value of verification and interpretation has increased. To that end, I take an ‘AI Positive’ posture, allowing students full access to any (free-tier) LLM for the ‘code-writing’ portions of the assignments. Because they have access to these tools, I in turn expect much more from them than I would have 10 years ago. In general, students seem to appreciate this Faustian bargain that I have thrust upon them1; for the instructor, it certainly is more rewarding (and more exhausting) than teaching basic programming.

This assignment is adapted from the second of four ‘mini-projects’ that replace traditional homework. In each of these, I work students through a full ‘soup-to-nuts’ data analysis project; early in the semester, I handle most of the steps for them, leaving only the final analysis and write-up for them to handle. As the semester progresses, I progressively remove this scaffolding and give them ownership of more and more of the ‘data science workflow’. Ultimately, this is intended to demonstrate the practices I encourage (and expect) them to use for their final project (in lieu of a traditional exam).

JOINing Data Sources

CautionLearning Objectives

The new learning objectives of this assignment are centered on combining data from different tables to allow novel insights that cannot be gleaned from any individual data source. By this point, students have already developed their skills manipulating data that has already been combined into one table, though they will build upon those skills in the later portions of this assignment.

If you ‘speak database’, this assignment basically covers SQL JOIN statements; it follows a ‘single-table’ lesson covering the R equivalent of SELECT, GROUP BY, WHERE/HAVING, and their kin.

At this point, students are familiar with the ideas of keys as a (hopefully) unique identifier within a data set. A prior in-class discussion has discussed examples of ID which are designed for use (e.g., CUNY EmplID) and those which are ‘good enough’ in a limited context (first name within a small class). We have also discussed how changing scope makes a formerly great ID insufficient (e.g., EmplID is a sufficient ID for post-2000 CUNY students, but would not suffice for registrar records (or the development office!) going back to the first days of the Free Academy).

Participants in this lesson need to know:

  • A key is a column (or combination of columns) that can be used to ‘connect’ records across different data sources.

  • We want keys to be unique and never missing (all records need a key)

  • Bad things happen when non-unique keys are used to combine data sets

  • Joining by identity is far more robust than joining by position.

    The first row of my data set may or may not match the first row of your data set, but Student 123456778 is likely to be the same across both.

The key, the whole key, and nothing by the key. So help me Codd.

Showtime!

Data Identification

For this assignment, we will be using data from the Internet Movie Database (IMDB), an online data base of all things cinema. Specifically, we will be using data from their Non-Commericial Dataset page. These files are made freely available by IMDb for non-commercial use.

CautionData Identification

I try to use a mix of easily understood data like this (Netflix Top 10, Spotify charts, etc) and more technical info (BLS statistics, Transit systems, etc) over the semester. Because STA9750 attracts students with such varied backgrounds, it is important to show them that - wherever they are going - the skills they develop in this class can be applied.

In my experience, students want evidence that the skills we want them to develop are actually useful, but they don’t actually demand applications to whatever they care about. Real applications to a real problem-even if not their problem- suffice.

Data Acquisition and Cleaning

CautionShow Everything

Here, I show students the entire workflow used to download, import, and clean up the five data sets that are used in this assignment. While this might seem overwhelming, I find it is important to expose them to the whole process, even if they are not responsible for it yet. This has several benefits:

  1. The students will submit a fully executable and reproducible document. It will start by automatically downloading the data file (if needed) and finish with their final figures and tables. This guarantees that the entire project is self-contained and can be read without reference to my original assignment.

    For students who want to use projects like this as the basis for a professional portfolio, this means their work truly stands on their own two feet.

  2. For later assignments, I can refer back to the code I share with them here as an example of practices they should follow when they are responsible for these initial set up steps.

  3. Psychologically, this makes it clear to students that they are interacting with the real thing, not a simplified and sanitized ‘textbook’ version of a problem. When there are weirdnesses and inconsistencies, they are just a fact of life to be dealt with - not a reason to complain and seek clarification.

The following code will automatically download and load these files into R:

library(tidyverse)
get_imdb_file <- function(fname){
    BASE_URL <- "https://datasets.imdbws.com/"
    fname_ext <- paste0(fname, ".tsv.gz")
    fs::dir_create("data")
    fname <- fs::path("data", fname_ext)
    if(!fs::file_exists(fname)){
        FILE_URL <- paste0(BASE_URL, fname_ext)
        download.file(FILE_URL, 
                      destfile = fname)
    }
    readr::read_tsv(fname, 
                    lazy=FALSE, 
                    show_col_types=FALSE, 
                    n_max=1000000, # Increase for more accurate results
                                   # We're currently using a subset for speed
                    na="\\N") |>
        mutate(across(ends_with("year"), as.numeric))
}

We want to perform the same filtering on our other TITLE_* tables. This is a rare use for the semi_join. Recall that a semi_join returns only values which have a match,but doesn’t actually add columns.

TITLE_BASICS <- TITLE_BASICS |>
    semi_join(TITLE_RATINGS, 
              join_by(tconst == tconst))

    semi_join(TITLE_RATINGS, 
              join_by(tconst == tconst))

    semi_join(TITLE_RATINGS, join_by(tconst == tconst))

Finally, let’s also restrict our attention to movies by filtering the TITLE_BASICS table to rows where titleType is "movie" and dropping shorts, tvMovies, tvSeries, tvShorts, etc. While I doubt that there are very many adult films with over 500 published reviews, let’s also discard films where isAdult is flagged as TRUE.

TITLE_BASICS <- TITLE_BASICS |> 
    filter_out(isAdult==1) |> 
    filter(titleType=="movie")|>
    select(-isAdult, -titleType)

In this case, there are sometimes several possible ways to join together a pair of tables–for instance, we could connect the TITLE_CREW table to the NAME_BASICS table by aligning director or writer. The following mental checklist will help you maximize the probability that you’re using the correct join for your analyses.5

NoteSelecting a Join

  1. What two pieces of information am I looking to connect? Answer(s): two columns from different tables. (If the columns are already in the same table, you don’t need a join.)

  2. What is the common feature(s) I can use to join together these two tables? Answer(s): one or more columns that have corresponding values in each table. It is not required for the columns to have the same name or the exact same set of values in both tables. (If there is not a natural pair of combining columns, you might need to introduce a third table to your analyis.)

  3. Join type:

    1. Do I want to guarantee use all of the rows from Table 1, filling in with missing values if no corresponding row is found in Table 2?
    2. Do I want to guarantee use all of the rows from Table 2, filling in with missing values if no corresponding row is found in Table 1?

    If “yes/yes”, you probably want a full_join; if “yes/no”, you probably want a left_join; if “no/yes”, you probably want a right_join; if “no/no”, you probably want an inner_join.

  4. Construct a potential join:

    JOIN_TYPE(TABLE_1_NAME, 
          TABLE_2_NAME, 
          join_by(TABLE_1_KEY_COLUMN == TABLE_2_KEY_COLUMN))

    Here, replace all values with the answers from your questions above. (We are not considering non-equality joins here.)

  5. Run your join and make sure you get no “many-to-many” or “many-to-one” warnings.

  6. Embed your join into your full analysis

For example, if we want to answer the question “Who played guitar in which bands?” using the band_members and band_instruments tables in the dplyr package, the join-design flowchart would go something like this:

  1. I want to connect the plays column from band_instruments to the band column of band_members.

  2. The common feature (“key”) connecting the band_instruments and band_members table is the name column, which has the same name and consistent values in both tables.

    1. I am fine dropping rows from the band_instruments table if I am unable to determine what band someone was a part of.
    2. I am fine dropping rows from the band_members table if I am unable to determine what instrument someone played.

    These suggest I want to use an inner_join.

  4. My candidate join is:

    library(dplyr)
inner_join(band_members, 
           band_instruments, 
           join_by(name == name))
    # A tibble: 2 × 3
  name  band    plays 
  <chr> <chr>   <chr> 
1 John  Beatles guitar
2 Paul  Beatles bass

  5. This runs without any warnings or other danger signs, so I’m good to proceed.

  6. I can use this join to find one guitarist in my data set with a known band:

    library(dplyr)
inner_join(band_members, 
           band_instruments, 
           join_by(name == name)) |>
    filter(plays == "guitar") 
    # A tibble: 1 × 3
  name  band    plays 
  <chr> <chr>   <chr> 
1 John  Beatles guitar

    so the one row in our result is John (Lennon) playing guitar in the Beatles. Sadly, our datasets were incomplete and we lost track of Keith Richards, guitarist in the Rolling Stones.

ImportantQuestion 1: Join Practice

Identify the joins necessary to answer the following questions. For each question, make sure to clearly justify:

  • What type of join is appropriate (and why)
  • Which column(s) should be used for the join. Note that the names may not be the same in each table, so be sure to carefully note the name in each table in case they differ.

You do not need to actually answer these, but make sure you know how you might begin to answer each question.

  1. What titles is the actor Harrison Ford most known for?

  2. Who is the most active director (most director credits) in this data set?

  3. Of titles with over 100,000 ratings, what is the most highly rated (highest average rating) title in this data set?

  4. Since 2020, what has been the average rating of a movie in the Musical genre?

Hint: It may be useful to create a “map” of which columns map to which tables before attempting these questions. While these can be quite formal and can be created with specialized software like http://dbdiagram.io, even some basic sketches on a scratch piece of paper are often quite clarifying.

TipExample Answer 1: Join Practice

  1. Because Harrison Ford is a person, we start with the NAME_BASICS table. Ideally, we could answer this question by looking at the knownForTitles column, but this has uninterpretable internal identifiers instead of actual names. To translate to actual names, we need to connect to the TITLE_BASICS table. In this case, we need to join knownForTitles from NAME_BASICS_LONGER to tconst in TITLE_BASICS. Here, I can use the LONGER name basics because I don’t mind the duplicates - I want multiple rows in my answers.

    In this case, a standard inner_join() is fine. We only are interested in the intersection of Harrison Ford’s most noteworthy titles and their names, so we are fine dropping unmatched values.

  2. Director credits can be inferred from the directors column of the TITLE_CREW data set. We want to map this to a name from the NAME_BASICS table. To align our two tables, we want TITLE_CREW’s directors to match NAME_BASICS’s nconst. As before, we are only interested in the intersection of these two tables, so an inner_join() will suffice.

Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)

CautionGuided Exploration

An important skill for students to learn is ‘data orienteering’, situating themselves within a new data set. While many approaches to this sort of Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) task have been proposed, my favorite is simple: ‘ask a bunch of questions and see what you find out.’ I pose warm-up questions to students to seed ideas for the questions they might ask to produce their final deliverable. My goal in these is to show students that there isn’t one right answer to any meaningful question.

For this demo, I’m keeping these relatively short, but this typically forms the bulk of the assignment.

Quantifying Success

Our goal is to proposal successful new movies. To do so, we need a way of measuring the success of a movie given only IMDb ratings.6 While there’s no “magic number” for success, it is logical to assume that a successful project will have both a high average IMDb rating, indicating quality, and a large number of ratings, indicating broad awareness in the public.

ImportantQuestion 2: Success Metric

Design a ‘success’ measure for IMDb entries, reflecting both quality and broad popular awareness.

Validate your success metric as follows:

  1. Choose the top 5-10 movies on your metric and confirm that they were indeed box office successes.
  2. Choose 3-5 movies with large numbers of IMDb votes that score poorly on your success metric and confirm that they are indeed of low quality.
  3. Choose a prestige actor or director and confirm that they have many projects with high scores on your success metric.
  4. Come up with a numerical threshold for a project to be a ‘success’; that is, determine a value \(v\) such that movies above \(v\) are all “solid” or better.
TipExample Answer 2: Success Metric

I am a particularly avaricious vulture, so my success metric will depend almost exclusively on box office revenue, which I will proxy with the quantity of IMDb ratings. I only want to use quality to avoid ‘review bombs’, where internet hordes rate a movie just to express their distaste even if they don’t see the film. To be clear, I have no qualms whatsoever about producing a film that everyone hates, as long as they pay me to hate it.

After a bit of experimentation, the following success score seems to work well:

\[\text{Success} = \frac{\text{Quantity of Ratings}}{1 + e^{-\text{Avg Rating}}}\]

To implement this in code, I need to join together my table of movies (TITLE_BASICS) with my table of ratings (TITLE_RATINGS). In this case, the join is straightforward as I can use the tconst column from both tables. An inner_join() works well here since I only am interested in looking at movies where I actually have sufficient info to compute my success metric.

Examining Success by Genre and Decade

Now that you have a working proxy for success, it’s time to look at trends in success over time

ImportantQuestion 3: Picking a Genre

Identify a good genre for your next film. You do not need to answer these questions precisely, but these are may help guide your thinking.

  • What was the genre with the most “successes” in each decade?
  • What genre consistently has the most “successes”? What genre used to reliably produced “successes” and has fallen out of favor?
  • What genre has produced the most “successes” since 2000? Does it have the highest success rate or does it only have a large number of successes because there are many productions in that genre?
  • What genre has become more popular in recent years?

Based on your findings, select a genre for your next project. Note that you may wish to avoid an “oversatured” genre; you just need to make the argument that your proposal is a good investment, not necessarily the most studio-produced focus-grouped committee-designed generic satisfying choice, so feel free to lean in to your own artistic preferences, as long as you can make an argument for them.

Analysis

CautionScaffold to the End

Once students are (hopefully) comfortable working with the data, I walk them through the rest of the analysis. The questions I ask here are typically intentionally a bit vague, giving students the ability to put their own stamp and express their own preferences in their analysis. By making these vague and soliciting preferences, I try to ensure that no students have the same answers at the end of the assignment. Then, when students participate in a ‘code review’ cycle, they can compare how they approached an open-ended prompt with their peers, instead only looking for binary ‘right/wrong’ answers. Personal preference isn’t the only way to make questions open ended, but it seems to make the point more effectively than other phrasings.

The Director

To get the ball rolling, you next want to line up some behind-the-camera talent for your production. Because directors are the hardest to recruit to a project9 and have strong opinions about the rest of the behind-the-camera crew, you are going to first identify a director to helm your film. You are new to the whole development game, so you plan on hiring a ‘bankable’ director who can’t miss in the genre: think Fincher in thrillers or Del Toro in Dark Fantasy.

ImportantQuestion 5: Finding Direction

Identify a director for your film. This should be someone with a track-record of successful films in your chosen genre. Ideally, they will not have worked in your genre recently - perhaps having a flop in a different genre - and you will be able to market your film as a ‘triumphant return to form’.

Presentation of Findings

Now that you have completed your analysis, write an “elevator pitch” of approximately 200-250 words for your proposed Hollywood project. This is the pitch you will bring to the studio head (your boss); if the studio head likes your pitch, you will be given a small sum of money to start securing the story rights and locking down tentative deals with key talent.

If your boss approves the pitch, you will need to have a brief trailer ready for the next quarterly earnings call. The marketing department has asked that you prepare a classic 90’s style teaser for them. Adapt the following cliched formula for your pitch.

General Remarks

As you approach this project, recall there are no right or wrong answers. You are exploring data looking for exciting and actionable findings. You have several key decisions to make and you can support them with data, but the decisions are ultimately yours. This project is an exercise both in the “nuts-and-bolts” of analyzing a large data set and in using data to inform and refine what is ultimately still a “gut feeling” qualitative business decision.

As you iterate on this project, you will see that seemingly small different choices can produce very different results. That’s ok! As data analysts, we are constantly faced with small and essentially arbitrary decisions. An important “meta-skill” is knowing which of these decisions radically change our findings and which are meaningless. (An arbitrary decision with no impact on the bottom line is harmless; an arbitrary decision that could entirely change the plan for the next ten years is a problem.) Our responsibility is to clearly communicate these choices to our partners and clients: then we can receive their feedback on which way they would like to proceed.

Working in tools like Quarto and R helps here: if we provide clean and reproducible code, it should be easy to modify to see how our final conclusions are changed. Graphics also play an essential role in this form of clear communication: a ‘point estimate’ like “Action A is the best” is far less interpretable than a chart showing the predicted outcomes of several different actions.

As you approach this project, focus on justifying and communicating the choices you make. Structure your argument to communicate both key findings and uncertainties around them. Think about how you can use both document structure (headings vs subsections) and graphics to communicate with both clarity and nuance.

Good luck!
